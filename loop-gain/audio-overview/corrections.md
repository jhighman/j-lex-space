# Corrections and framing for the audio overview

*A source to add to the Notebook before regenerating the audio overview.
It states facts the first overview got wrong or left out, in plain
sentences, so the hosts can rely on it. Written 2026-10-28.*

## Who did this

Two people did this work, not a community. Jeff Highman built the models,
the benches, the proofs and the piece, working with an AI assistant
(Claude). Alexandra Krížová wrote the two concept briefs, extended the
second with the allostatic reading, set the translation test every result
was read against, and is credited at her own request as critical reviewer
and development reviewer. The two deliberately use different AI
assistants so they do not share one set of blind spots. There is no
international research community, no mixed-education collective, and no
gigabytes of logs. There is a public GitHub repository, a week of work,
and a standing rule that nothing is evaluated by the process that
produced it.

## What the claims are about

Every finding and every theorem is about a model. None is a claim about a
person. The simulated sessions last two to three minutes, or twelve in
one test, not hours. "Attention", "calm", "agency" and "the minutes" are
four numbers in a model that wear down by stated rules. Whether a feed
does anything like this to a person is a hypothesis, and the sources say
so each time. Whether a person's suppression loop has a gain above one,
and what it is, is a question for a person and a measurement, not for a
bench. The correct phrasing is always "in the model" or "in the
simulation".

## Agency, correctly

In the model, agency falls with the share of the stream that is the
reader's own echo and falls a little with every move she makes. It rises
only when a like on something she wanted actually worked, or when she
uses the one permanent act, the Empty Bin. It does not come back when her
echoes age out, because being buried is not agency. It is not a ratio of
proactive to reactive choices. The feed does not "probe boundaries" after
a mute; a mute removes a channel.

## The feed's result, correctly

Sweeping the interval between arrivals from twelve seconds down to a
third of a second, every persona's end state held flat down to about 3.5
seconds between arrivals and then slid, over roughly a factor of ten in
the interval. No cliff. The knee has a reason: in the model attention
recovers only after three seconds of quiet, and the share of gaps long
enough for that is a continuous function of the interval, one above five
seconds and zero below two. The attention curves track that function at a
correlation of 0.90 to 0.997. This is about the model's attention
variable, not human attention.

## The exclusion result, correctly, including the failure

The team wrote down, before any run, that the exclusion loop has a
reproduction number R0 equal to propensity times gain times the return
share, and predicted a critical point at a propensity of 0.8. The
pre-registered sweep could not see it: the verdict rule, written for the
feed's bounded quantity, read "slides", because a quantity that diverges
grows smoothly through its critical value in a three-minute session. That
hypothesis failed under its own test, and the failure is kept in the
record at full length. A second test, labelled post-hoc, asked whether the
end state depends on how long the session ran: below 0.8 a twelve-minute
session ends where a three-minute one does; at 0.9 it ends sixty times
higher. The turn is at 0.8, where the number said. Without gain there is
no critical point at any propensity.

## The fourth frame, correctly

Lex's allostatic load was in the model from the start, in three frames:
fatigue (load lowers the capacity to exclude), reactivity (load raises
it), and none. The fourth frame came from Wegner's ironic process theory
(spelled Wegner): load leaves the monitor running while the operator
tires, so in the model load raises the return rate. The prediction, made
first, was that the critical point would move only a little, and it did.
The prediction that the frame would be small was wrong by an order of
magnitude: R0 says whether the loop runs away; the return rate says how
fast. Fatigue, by contrast, bounded the exterior and the body settled at
half its load rather than paying to the cap.

## The proofs, correctly

Twenty-one theorems in Lean 4, core only, no library. They say what the
rules entail: the band cannot be kept flat by acting; the allostatic floor
never comes down; a moment's calm deficit persists as structure; agency
never rises without a like that worked or an emptying; supercritical mass
is unbounded and subcritical mass is bounded; the exterior never
decreases under the viewer's acts; permanent exclusion lowers R0 without
removing the threshold; a higher return rate never lowers R0 and R0 never
exceeds propensity times gain. The proofs do not establish the slope or
the critical point; those came from simulation and a derivation. They do
not prove anything about a philosophy or a person.

## The piece, correctly

*Loop Gain* (not Loop Game): three minutes, three movements. The line, the
loop, the plate. The loop's resonance at 870 Hz is placed by hand, as a
room has a resonance, and the sources say so; a soft clip stands where a
speaker's ceiling would be. Two of Lex's perceptual readings from the
review are in the piece: Bridson's growth as the exterior and Atlas as
the piece's own map. The movements are cut by the clock, not by the
sound, which the sources name as a compromise.

## The reverse arrow, correctly

A soundtrack that lags the pulse and leads it down is a hypothesis
nobody has run. The sources say exactly that. The published effect of
tempo on heart rate is modest, over minutes. No proof in the repository
reaches it; the Lean theorems are about the two models, neither of which
contains a body. It is not "mathematically sound", not "proven", and not
"entirely possible" on the strength of anything here. It is the next
experiment, and it needs a person and a sensor.

## The phrase "do your own research"

The paper's closing section is about what a small team can do with a
laptop, seeded models, predictions written down before the runs, a proof
assistant, a collaborator who reads against you, and a record that keeps
failures at full length. It is advice, offered with the week's own
failures as the evidence. It is not a movement.
