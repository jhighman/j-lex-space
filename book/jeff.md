## Am I Useful?

*12 August 2026*

Am I useful? Am I purposeful? Do I have a way to take what I know and be useful
to somebody? This is loop one.

Can I learn something? How do I learn something? How do I explain something I
learned? How is that useful? This is loop two.

Am I connecting with someone? Do I understand them? Do they understand me? What
is the purpose of this relationship? This is loop three.

What is confusing? Why is it confusing? How can I break it down? What can I
solve today, what goes on the backlog to solve for tomorrow. This is loop four.

What compels me? What am I attracted to? What is beautiful? How can I move
toward it faithfully? This is counter loop one.

What or who stands in my way, how do I overcome, what is success, what is
failure, how do I succeed? This is counter loop two.

What or who do I need to protect? How do I protect them? How can my protection
not get in the way? This is counter loop three.

What am I learning, how am I growing, what am I leaving behind, what endings are
necessary now, what endings are necessary in the future, what beginnings are
now, and what are in the future? This is counter loop four.

| | Loop | Fundamental question | Counter-loop | Fundamental question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Purpose / Utility | How can I be useful? | Desire / Beauty | What is worth moving toward? |
| 2 | Learning / Knowledge | How do I understand and apply? | Agency / Struggle | What must I overcome? |
| 3 | Relationship / Understanding | How do we understand one another? | Protection / Stewardship | What am I responsible for preserving? |
| 4 | Problem-solving / Order | What can I make less confusing today? | Growth / Transition | What must change, end, or begin? |

The counter-loops aren't opposites. They prevent the primary loops from becoming
pathological.

Claims:

Utility without beauty becomes mere productivity. You can become extraordinarily
useful while moving toward nothing you actually value.

Learning without struggle becomes accumulation. You know more and more without
testing whether the knowledge survives contact with resistance.

Relationship without protection can become belonging for its own sake.
Protection introduces responsibility — but your last question is crucial: How
can my protection not get in the way? That recognizes that stewardship can
become possession.

Problem-solving without transition becomes maintenance. You get extremely good
at clearing the backlog of a life or system that perhaps ought to end.

It works in reverse as well; you might call these axioms:

Beauty without usefulness can become fantasy.
Struggle without learning becomes conquest.
Protection without relationship becomes control.
Transition without problem-solving becomes perpetual reinvention.

So these aren't really eight independent loops. They're four coupled
oscillations:

- Useful ↔ Beautiful
- Knowing ↔ Overcoming
- Connecting ↔ Protecting
- Solving ↔ Becoming

At a process level, continuously negotiating self, world, other, and time.

Loop one locates the self in the world: What can I contribute?
Loop two updates the model of the world: What can I learn?
Loop three negotiates the boundary between self and other: Can we understand
each other?
Loop four acts upon the world as encountered: What can I resolve?

Then the counter-loops introduce direction: what attracts me, what resists me,
what I must preserve, and what time requires me to relinquish.

That's why counter-loop four feels different from everything preceding it. It
contains temporality. Once you ask *What am I leaving behind?*, the architecture
can no longer merely recurse. It has to evolve.

Sometimes evolution is emergence, and a more authentic self is revealed.
Sometimes evolution is direction; it informs where I am going and who I am
taking with me. Sometimes evolution is conviction; it's moving through something
difficult.

In the 42-day intellectual exercise, I learned things that I would not have
otherwise learned. Some of these things had confounded me for years. Who is my
brother? What is his ethos? How do I admire him? How are we the same? I can do
this now fluidly because I have learned to read epistemologically. Another
example. How would I describe my father honorably and yet honestly?

For me, I live in the narrative, and language appears primarily as an interface
between states. I know something → explain → someone else can use it. I
understand something → communicate → someone else understands me. I encounter
confusion → decompose → construct a tractable representation.

I suppose if there's one overarching sense of purpose that I feel compelled,
it's to teach others how to do this themselves, find others that have similar
systems, and return to loop one. I suspect that we gravitate to occupations that
allow us to repeatedly run the cognitive loops that feel most natural and
meaningful to us.

A job is, in that sense, a socially useful place to perform your preferred form
of cognition. In this context, I don't mean a job to be working for a company.
Oftentimes, the job is what we choose to do voluntarily. Our economic job is
often something maligned, but necessary. Society inverts value to economics, and
this reduces purpose.

A surgeon, for example, might be Utility + Resolution + Protection.
A scientist might be Learning + Resolution + Beauty.
A therapist might be Connection + Protection + Transition.
An entrepreneur might be Beauty + Resistance + Resolution + Transition.
A teacher might be Learning + Connection + Protection + Transition.

And two people can therefore occupy exactly the same profession for completely
different cognitive reasons.

One lawyer loves the intellectual decomposition of a difficult argument: Loop 4.
Another loves winning: Counter-loop 2.
Another is defending vulnerable people: Counter-loop 3.
Another loves understanding how rules and institutions evolve: Loop 2 +
Counter-loop 4.

Same credential. Same occupation. Completely different internal reward system.

That may explain something career assessments often miss: **occupation isn't
identity; it's habitat.** We are searching for environments that repeatedly
generate the kinds of problems our minds find rewarding to process.

And that gives us a more interesting conception of occupational fit:

> Fit = how frequently the environment invokes your dominant loops + how much
> agency you have to complete them.

The second part matters enormously.

Someone whose dominant loop is confusion → decomposition → resolution will
probably become miserable in an organization where they can identify problems
but aren't allowed to fix them.

Someone driven by learning → explanation → usefulness may hate a role where
they're constantly learning but their knowledge never affects anything.

Someone driven by connection → understanding can be surrounded by people all day
and nevertheless feel occupationally starved if every relationship is
transactional.

Someone oriented toward protection may initially flourish in governance or
security and eventually become miserable if the institution rewards protecting
the bureaucracy rather than protecting people.

It also explains career evolution. The loops probably aren't equally weighted
throughout a lifetime.

Early career can be dominated by: Can I learn? Can I succeed? Can I prove that
I'm useful?

Mid-career can shift toward: Can I solve consequential problems? Can I protect
something important? Can I make what I know useful to others?

And later life introduces Counter-loop 4 with considerably more force: What am I
leaving behind? What should continue without me? What needs to end? What am I
becoming now?

Which means the classic "midlife career crisis" may sometimes be something much
simpler: **the occupation hasn't changed, but my dominant loop has.** The
habitat that once fed the mind no longer does. It's time to emerge.

But what good is it to emerge if the only thing that emerges is you? For me,
that's just not good enough. I feel like it's my job to bring others along. I
see friends get stuck in places that I feel like I understand, but can't make my
understanding palatable or compelling. I see principles, choices, and
consequences as being favorable to a disciplined mind, but torturous to the mind
that has convinced itself of its own virtue, salvation, or purpose. For me, the
ultimate difference is that I do not define my purpose; it is given to me by
God. This releases me from the loop and washes me white as snow (but only for a
moment).

We may choose careers partly by discovering, often accidentally, which loops we
want to spend our lives running. Perhaps vocation is the intersection between
the loops we are intrinsically compelled to run and the loops the world is
willing to reward us for running. That distinction between occupation and
vocation might be worth keeping. An occupation pays you for a cognitive pattern.
A vocation makes that pattern feel purposeful.

Even emergence can become an extraordinarily sophisticated form of
self-occupation. I emerge. I become wiser. I become integrated. I understand
myself. I transcend my former limitations.

A disciplined mind can say: I may be wrong. My choices have consequences. My
intentions do not determine those consequences. Therefore I must submit my
beliefs to correction. But once I make my own virtue, salvation, enlightenment,
intelligence, victimhood, ideology (or even my purpose) the proof of my
correctness, correction becomes existentially threatening.

In this case, evidence isn't merely contradicting a proposition anymore. It is
contradicting me.

Within my Christian understanding, that isn't another answer inside the loops.
It establishes an authority outside them.

I am no longer required to derive your worth from usefulness. I don't have to
know enough. I don't have to be understood. I don't have to solve everything. I
don't have to possess everything beautiful. I don't have to defeat every
obstacle. I don't have to successfully protect everyone. I don't even have to
successfully become the person I imagined becoming.

None of those things establishes the fundamental claim about who I am or why I
exist.

Grace is not the successful completion of the architecture. It is precisely the
thing the architecture cannot produce for itself.

I can't reason myself clean, discipline myself clean, emerge myself clean, or
even make myself useful enough to become clean.

What I can do with others: I can teach. I can witness. I can protect. I can make
difficult ideas intelligible. I can love someone stubbornly. I can walk beside
them. But I cannot emerge on their behalf.

I am responsible for faithfulness, not sovereignty over the outcome. This is the
divine irony. Sovereignty is granted by God, not claimed by acts. All that is
given will be taken away. Knowing the purpose in this inverts the fear of
judgment, but instead the joy of it.
