What does sound look like?

A metal plate vibrating at one of its resonant frequencies. Sand is thrown off the parts that move and comes to rest on the lines that stay still. Each tone draws its own figure, on a round plate like the ones Hans Jenny filmed or a square one like Chladni's.

Resonant tones

Nodal lines

At a resonance the plate splits into regions moving in opposite directions. Between them run lines that hardly move at all. The sand ends up there, so the figure is a map of the stillness inside the sound.

Between tones

Off resonance the plate barely responds and the sand stays where it is. Slide the frequency slowly: one figure holds, then dissolves, then a new one gathers as the next resonance arrives.

Lines and rings

On a round plate the still places are straight lines through the centre and circles around it. Each figure is a count of both: how many lines cross the plate and how many rings sit inside it. Higher tones add more of each.

Hans Jenny

The Swiss physician Hans Jenny (1904–1972) spent the 1960s vibrating plates and membranes covered with sand, powders, pastes and liquids, holding each tone steady with crystal oscillators. He filmed what formed and named the field cymatics in his 1967 book Kymatik, building on Ernst Chladni's bowed plates of 1787.

Model: the round plate uses Bessel functions, the mathematics of a vibrating drumhead with a free edge; the square plate uses two crossed standing waves. Both give the right kind of figure. The pitches are chosen for listening and are not measured from a real plate.
